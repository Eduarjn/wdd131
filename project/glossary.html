The words in your carrier proposal

Telecom runs on acronyms, and a quote written in them is hard to argue with. These are the twenty terms that decide what you pay and what your calls sound like, written the way an engineer would explain them to a friend who signs the cheques.

Browse the terms

SIP
Session Initiation Protocol. The language phones use to set up and tear down a call. It carries no audio at all – it negotiates who is calling whom and which codec both ends will use, then hands the conversation to RTP. When a call connects but has no sound, SIP worked and RTP did not.
SIP trunk
The link between your phone system and a carrier, sold as a bundle of simultaneous channels. It replaces the physical copper lines a business used to rent. You buy a channel count, not a line count, and that number is the one the calculator produces.
PBX or PABX
Private Branch Exchange: the switchboard that belongs to your company. It decides that extension 204 rings at a particular desk, plays the hold music, and routes outside calls to the trunk. Modern ones are software, often the open source Asterisk or FreeSWITCH.
Channel, or concurrent call
One conversation in progress. A trunk with eighteen channels can carry eighteen conversations; the nineteenth caller gets a busy signal. Carriers bill mostly by this number, which is why sizing it correctly is worth real money every month.
DID or DDI
Direct Inward Dialing. A public phone number that rings a specific extension without going through a receptionist. DIDs and channels are priced separately and are unrelated: a company can own two hundred DIDs and still need only twelve channels, because not everyone is on the phone at once.
Erlang
The unit of telephone traffic, named after the Danish mathematician Agner Krarup Erlang. One erlang is one circuit occupied continuously for one hour. Two hundred calls of three minutes in an hour is ten erlangs, because that is ten hours of talking inside one hour of clock time.
Busy hour
The sixty minutes of the day when traffic peaks, usually mid morning or just after lunch. Systems are sized for this hour and not for the daily average, because an average hides exactly the moment the system has to survive.
Grade of service
The share of calls you accept losing in the busy hour, written as a decimal. A grade of service of 0.01 means one caller in a hundred hears a busy signal. It is a business decision rather than a technical one: a hospital and a pizza shop should not choose the same number.
Erlang B
The formula that converts traffic and a grade of service into a channel count. It assumes a blocked caller gives up rather than redialling immediately, which suits trunk sizing. Its sibling Erlang C assumes callers wait in a queue, and is what you use for contact centre staffing.
CPS
Calls per second, the rate at which new calls are set up. Distinct from concurrent calls: an auto dialler can generate high CPS with few simultaneous conversations. Carriers cap CPS separately, and hitting that ceiling looks like random call failures rather than busy signals.
Codec
Coder and decoder: the method that turns sound into data and back. The choice sets both the bandwidth per call and the ceiling on how good the call can sound. G.711 sends everything uncompressed, G.729 compresses eight to one.
RTP
Real-time Transport Protocol, which carries the audio once SIP has set the call up. It runs over UDP, so a late packet is simply dropped rather than resent – audio that arrives too late to play is worse than useless.
Latency
How long a packet takes to travel one way. Under 150 milliseconds nobody notices. Past about 250 the natural rhythm of conversation breaks and both people start talking over each other, then apologising.
Jitter
The variation in arrival time between packets that were sent evenly spaced. Voice does not mind a slow network nearly as much as an unpredictable one, because steady delay can be absorbed and unpredictable delay cannot.
Jitter buffer
A small holding area at the receiving end that collects packets and releases them at an even rate, trading a little extra delay for smooth audio. Too small and the audio stutters; too large and the call feels like a satellite link.
Packet loss
Packets that never arrive. One percent is usually inaudible because the codec fills the gap by guessing; three percent sounds like a bad mobile connection. Loss that arrives in bursts is far more damaging than the same percentage spread evenly.
MOS
Mean Opinion Score, from one to five, produced by asking listeners to rate call quality. It is a human measurement rather than a technical one. Above 4.0 is good, 3.6 is the point where people start complaining, and nothing over a telephone reaches 5.
SBC
Session Border Controller: the firewall built for voice. It sits between your system and the carrier, hides your internal topology, fixes the NAT problems that break audio, and blocks the toll fraud attempts that arrive within hours of a system appearing online.
NAT traversal
The workarounds that let a phone behind a home router receive audio. SIP was designed before address translation was universal and puts internal addresses inside its messages, which the far end cannot reach. STUN, ICE and an SBC exist to repair this.
E.164
The international standard that makes a number unambiguous worldwide: a plus sign, country code, then the national number, to a maximum of fifteen digits. Storing numbers this way avoids the classic bug where a call works from the office and fails from abroad.
QoS and DSCP
Quality of Service: telling the network that voice packets go first. DSCP is the marking that carries the request, and voice normally uses the value named Expedited Forwarding. It only works where you control the equipment; the public internet ignores your markings.
Failover
What happens when the primary trunk stops answering. Systems send a periodic SIP OPTIONS message as a heartbeat and switch to a second carrier when the replies stop. Worth testing deliberately, because the first real failure is a poor time to discover the backup was never configured.
